# Boston BERDO + Veolia District-Steam Research

Date: 2026-06-04
Purpose: Test the causal premise of the ConEd threshold-proximity feature — does carbon-mandate pressure actually drive district-steam customers off steam, or do they comply through other pathways?

Note on operator: Veolia sold its US district-energy assets to Antin Infrastructure Partners in December 2019. The Boston system is now operated as **Vicinity Energy**. "Veolia Boston steam" and "Vicinity Boston steam" refer to the same physical network.

## Verdict (one paragraph)

**Confidence: Low-to-moderate, leaning AGAINST the assumed mechanism.** The evidence available today does not support a strong causal arrow from carbon-mandate pressure → district-steam disconnection. Three things stand out: (1) BERDO 2.0's first compliance year is 2025, with first reports not due until August 2026, so the natural experiment has barely begun and there is no observable wave of disconnections to count; (2) the dominant operator response has been "decarbonize the steam in place" via eSteam (electric boilers + heat pumps at central plants), explicitly recognized as zero-emission under BERDO regulations, which gives covered customers a way to comply *without leaving the network*; and (3) where building owners are actively retrofitting, the trade press names electrification of on-site gas boilers, envelope upgrades, RECs, and ACPs far more often than "drop district steam" as the path being taken. The only widely reported eSteam offtakers so far are Emerson College and IQHQ — institutional/developer customers staying on the system, not leaving it. Mass General Hospital, the most-quoted institutional steam customer, has explicitly *not* committed to eSteam and is studying heat pumps but has not announced disconnection. If anything, the Boston signal so far is: under carbon pressure, large institutional steam customers are *negotiating with the steam utility for green product*, not exiting. That is the opposite of what a "threshold-proximity → disconnect" feature assumes.

## Evidence

### Q1: BERDO as LL97 analog

- Finding [Confirmed]: Both BERDO 2.0 and LL97 are building-performance standards that set declining per-area carbon caps on large existing buildings, with monetary penalties for exceedance. BERDO ACP is $234/metric ton CO2e; LL97 penalty is $268/metric ton — structurally and numerically close. Both target net-zero by 2050.
  Source: https://nerej.com/greener-skylines-ahead-berdo-2-0-and-ll-97-part-iii
  Source: https://nerej.com/greener-skylines-ahead-berdo-2-0-and-local-law-97-part-2

- Finding [Confirmed]: BERDO 2.0 covers residential 15+ units / non-res 20,000+ sf; first emissions compliance period started 1 Jan 2025 for the larger tier (35+ units / 35,000+ sf), with first emissions reports due 15 May 2026 (extended to 15 Aug 2026). Smaller tier compliance starts 2030.
  Source: https://www.boston.gov/departments/environment/berdo
  Source: https://envigilance.com/energy-monitoring/berdo/

- Finding [Confirmed]: Per-day fines for ongoing non-compliance: $1,000/day for 35,000+ sf buildings, $300/day for 20,000–35,000 sf buildings. The Boston Environment Department can place liens on property for unpaid exceedance fees.
  Source: https://envigilance.com/energy-monitoring/berdo/

- Finding [Confirmed]: BERDO compliance pathways are diverse: direct emissions reductions, electrification, on-site renewables, RECs (MA Class I), alternative compliance payments, building-portfolio compliance, individual compliance schedules, hardship plans, and use of BERDO-recognized decarbonized district energy. This is a wider menu of off-ramps than is sometimes assumed for LL97.
  Source: https://www.boston.gov/departments/environment/berdo
  Source: https://www.abettercity.org/news-and-events/blog/final-berdo-regulations,-key-takeaways,-and-next-steps

- Finding [Suggestive]: LL97 historically permitted only ~10% offsets in the first compliance period; BERDO doesn't allow offsets but does allow RECs and ACPs. Net effect: comparable structures, with BERDO arguably *more* flexible on financial/alt-compliance paths.
  Source: https://nerej.com/greener-skylines-ahead-berdo-2-0-and-local-law-97-by-roselin-osser

- Structural caveat: Boston's housing stock skews more heavily to natural gas as the primary heating fuel than Manhattan's, where district steam concentration is higher in core CBD. So "what Boston buildings do under BERDO" is mostly a story about gas-boiler buildings, not steam buildings. The Vicinity-served subset is the right slice to look at, and it's small (~245 buildings).
  Source: https://emergentenergy.us/blog/berdo-20-and-bostons-building-performance-standards-what-pro

### Q2: Veolia/Vicinity Boston system trajectory

- Finding [Confirmed]: Vicinity's Boston system serves ~245 buildings, 71.4 million sf, across 29 miles of pipe. Customer mix is commercial, healthcare, government, institutional, hospitality (Four Seasons, Northeastern University, Boston Medical Center, New England Aquarium named).
  Source: https://www.vicinityenergy.us/locations/boston/

- Finding [Confirmed]: Vicinity (under Antin Infrastructure Partners ownership) has invested in a 42 MW electric boiler (commissioned Nov 2024) and is constructing a 35 MW river-source ammonia heat pump complex at Kendall Square (targeted 2028), with plans to install 100–150 MW of electric boiler capacity total by 2028, enough to serve up to 75% of current steam load.
  Source: https://www.vicinityenergy.us/press-releases/vicinity-energy-commences-construction-of-industrial-scale-heat-pump-complex-in-partnership-with-dco-energy-and-everllence/
  Source: https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/clean-industry/bostons-century-old-steam-heat-system-is-getting-a-low-carbon-makeover

- Finding [Suggestive]: Vicinity is being positioned by its PE owner for growth, not wind-down. The Boston Globe profile of Vicinity's leadership describes Antin as "fully on board" with expansion plans, with new contracts being negotiated. New customers (Emerson College, IQHQ in Fenway) are being added, not just retained.
  Source: https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/04/20/business/vicinity-bill-decroce/

- Finding [Unknown]: I could not find a public datapoint stating Vicinity's net Boston customer count or steam volume change since BERDO 2.0 was enacted in 2021. There is no obvious public source that tracks year-over-year disconnects. ConEd's own internal customer data would be the analogous figure for NYC and is also non-public.

### Q3: Convert off steam vs comply another way (CORE)

This is where the evidence most clearly points away from the "carbon pressure → disconnect" causal arrow:

- Finding [Confirmed]: BERDO 2.0 regulations explicitly recognize Vicinity's eSteam as carbon-free. That means a covered Boston steam customer can comply with BERDO by switching their *steam product* from conventional to eSteam — without disconnecting from the network, without on-site retrofits, and without ACPs. This is structurally a "stay on steam, comply via supplier decarbonization" pathway.
  Source: https://www.vicinityenergy.us/blog/how-district-energy-is-helping-commercial-buildings-meet-bostons-berdo-2-0-requirements/
  Source: https://www.powermag.com/how-building-owners-can-achieve-performance-standards-with-district-energy/

- Finding [Confirmed]: As of late 2023, WBUR reported that "only one building developer has signed up for the electric boiler steam so far." Adoption is slow, but the slowness cuts both ways — it also means almost no one is rushing to *leave* steam either.
  Source: https://www.wbur.org/news/2023/12/27/massachusetts-cambridge-boston-net-zero-building-heat-systems

- Finding [Confirmed]: Mass General Hospital — explicitly a Vicinity steam customer with steam responsible for 12% of campus emissions — has *not* committed to eSteam and is exploring heat pumps "very effectively." This is a hedge, not a disconnect, and it predates any BERDO deadline pressure.
  Source: https://www.wbur.org/news/2023/12/27/massachusetts-cambridge-boston-net-zero-building-heat-systems

- Finding [Confirmed]: Emerson College's commitment is to heat its *entire Boston campus* using eSteam — i.e., greater steam reliance, not less. IQHQ's Fenway development is similarly using eSteam from inception. Both are "stay on / expand on steam" stories driven by carbon pressure.
  Source: https://www.clf.org/blog/clean-steam-ahead-boston-pioneers-innovative-heating-tech/
  Source: https://www.vicinityenergy.us/press-releases/vicinity-energy-delivers-esteam-first-carbon-free-thermal-energy-solution-in-u-s/

- Finding [Unknown]: I could not find a single named Boston building publicly identified as having dropped district steam to comply with BERDO. Absence of evidence isn't proof of absence, but the trade press has been actively covering BERDO compliance stories and no such case has surfaced.

- Finding [Suggestive]: General BERDO compliance commentary in Banker & Tradesman and Vicinity/CLF/Canary coverage emphasizes three modes — retrofits, RECs, ACPs — and does *not* mention "drop district steam" as a common pathway. The IDEA presentation series on Boston/NYC steam network electrification is framed around helping *customer buildings comply on-network*, not modeling attrition.
  Source: https://bankerandtradesman.com/boston-developers-owners-face-the-cost-of-berdo-compliance/
  Source: https://learn.districtenergy.org/products/accelerating-downtown-decarbonization-with-district-energy-part-1-electrification-strategies-for-downtown-district-steam-networks-in-boston-san-francisco-and-new-york-city

### Q4: Compliance pathways being chosen

- Finding [Confirmed]: The named pathways in BERDO compliance coverage are, in approximate order of how often they appear in coverage:
  1. On-site retrofits: heat pumps replacing on-site gas boilers, envelope upgrades, controls/commissioning. The Vicinity-served slice is a minority of Boston BERDO buildings — most BERDO compliance discussion is about getting buildings off natural gas, not off district steam.
  2. RECs (MA Class I) — explicitly allowed; Boston extended REC eligibility to credits generated Jan 2024–June 2026 to cover the 2025 standard.
  3. Alternative Compliance Payments at $234/ton.
  4. Switching to eSteam where Vicinity service is available.
  5. Portfolio compliance / individual compliance schedules / hardship plans.
  Source: https://www.boston.gov/departments/environment/berdo
  Source: https://bankerandtradesman.com/boston-developers-owners-face-the-cost-of-berdo-compliance/
  Source: https://stanwichenergy.com/insights/berdo-2.0-navigating-bostons-enhanced-building-emissions-ordinance

- Finding [Suggestive]: For Vicinity-served buildings specifically, "switch to eSteam" is the lowest-friction option (no capital outlay, no service disruption) and Vicinity is actively marketing it as the BERDO compliance product. RECs partially help cover electricity-side emissions but don't address steam emissions; ACPs become an ongoing operating expense.
  Source: https://www.vicinityenergy.us/blog/how-district-energy-is-helping-commercial-buildings-meet-bostons-berdo-2-0-requirements/

- Finding [Unknown]: I could not find a quantitative breakdown — survey, city report, or analyst estimate — of how Boston building owners are actually splitting between these pathways. That would normally come from the first round of BERDO compliance filings (May/Aug 2026), which haven't yet been processed and published.

### Q5: Building-type differences

- Finding [Suggestive]: Large institutional Vicinity customers cluster around eSteam and on-network decarbonization, not exit. The named eSteam adopters (Emerson College, IQHQ life-sciences development) and the named non-adopter (MGH, exploring its own pathway but still on steam) are all institutional. The big-institution pattern in coverage is "explore options on the network plus auxiliary heat pumps," not "switch entire campus to heat pumps and disconnect."
  Source: https://www.wbur.org/news/2023/12/27/massachusetts-cambridge-boston-net-zero-building-heat-systems
  Source: https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/04/20/business/vicinity-bill-decroce/

- Finding [Suggestive]: MATEP — the cogen plant serving Longwood Medical Area hospitals — is separately operated (ENGIE/Axium since 2018) and is decarbonizing in place, not being shut down by the hospitals. The hospitals it serves are committing to 2050 net-zero but via shared central plant decarbonization, not by leaving the central plant.
  Source: https://www.engie-na.com/longwood_district_energy/
  Source: https://cleanthepeakma.org/matep/

- Finding [Suggestive]: Boston Medical Center has cut emissions 96% since 2011 via cogeneration, PPAs and campus consolidation — i.e., kept central thermal plant, decarbonized the inputs. No reported disconnection from district steam.
  Source: https://us.noharm.org/news/boston-hospitals-maintain-progress-reducing-ghg-emissions

- Finding [Unknown]: I found no comparable evidence on small commercial Boston steam customers' behavior. They are a smaller share of the Vicinity load and get much less coverage. The implicit ConEd claim that institutional customers rarely disconnect is *consistent* with the Boston signal, but Boston doesn't independently prove or disprove the small-commercial side.

### Q6: Timing maturity

- Finding [Confirmed]: The experiment has barely run. BERDO 2.0 was enacted Oct 2021. First compliance period is calendar year 2025. First emissions filings due 15 May 2026, extended by the BERDO Review Board to 15 Aug 2026. Third-party verification adds further lag. So as of June 2026, the first wave of compliance results is in early stages of being filed but not yet publicly analyzed.
  Source: https://envigilance.com/energy-monitoring/berdo/
  Source: https://www.boston.gov/departments/environment/berdo

- Finding [Suggestive]: Estimates cited in trade coverage suggest roughly 70% of larger-tier buildings will be compliant for 2025 and ~30% will need ACPs, RECs, or retrofits to reach compliance. These are projections, not measured outcomes.
  Source: https://www.boston.gov/news/take-action-if-your-building-may-be-out-compliance-berdo

- Implication: Any "Boston shows what happens" claim about BERDO-driven steam attrition is currently a *leading-indicators* claim — announced plans, RFPs, signed eSteam contracts, retrofit permits — not an outcomes claim about who actually disconnected. The 2026–2028 window (post first-cycle filings + heat pump complex coming online in 2028) is when we'd expect the real signal to show up.

### Q7: Direct NYC-to-Boston commentary

- Finding [Confirmed]: The IDEA "Accelerating Downtown Decarbonization with District Energy" series explicitly puts Boston (Vicinity), NYC (ConEd), and San Francisco district steam side by side. The framing in those sessions is *operator decarbonization to retain customers under BPS pressure*, not customer attrition modeling. ConEd's own April 2024 "Electrification Challenges in Dense Urban Environments Study" similarly assumes the steam network is a long-lived asset to be greened, not run off.
  Source: https://learn.districtenergy.org/products/accelerating-downtown-decarbonization-with-district-energy-part-1-electrification-strategies-for-downtown-district-steam-networks-in-boston-san-francisco-and-new-york-city
  Source: https://documents.dps.ny.gov/public/Common/ViewDoc.aspx?DocRefId=%7B70710994-0000-CE3D-99A9-59EDFB977282%7D

- Finding [Suggestive]: The NEREJ "Greener Skylines" series is the most direct LL97-vs-BERDO comparison aimed at real-estate practitioners. It treats district steam under both regimes as a compliance complication for buildings with fossil-fueled steam — but its recommended responses are efficiency/retrofit/RECs/ACPs, not "disconnect from steam."
  Source: https://nerej.com/greener-skylines-ahead-berdo-2-0-and-ll-97-part-iii
  Source: https://nerej.com/greener-skylines-ahead-berdo-2-0-and-local-law-97-part-2

- Finding [Suggestive]: POWER Magazine's analysis explicitly argues that district-energy customers under BPS get *cost and disruption advantages* by staying connected to a decarbonizing central plant rather than going through individual building-level retrofits — i.e., the trade view actively pushes against the "BPS → disconnect" hypothesis.
  Source: https://www.powermag.com/how-building-owners-can-achieve-performance-standards-with-district-energy/

## Implications

### For threshold-proximity feature

The causal premise that "LL97 penalty pressure → steam conversion" is, at best, **not well supported by the Boston analog at this point in time**. The Boston evidence so far points to three responses that all leave the customer ON district steam:

1. The steam utility decarbonizes its product (eSteam / electrified central plant) and the building complies by buying the new product.
2. The building uses RECs / ACPs to cover residual exceedance — financial compliance, no infrastructure change.
3. The building does on-site efficiency and partial electrification of non-steam loads, but keeps steam for the legacy thermal load.

Disconnection — actually leaving the network — is conspicuously absent from public Boston cases, including among the institutional customers whose footprint and emissions exposure make them the obvious test case.

For the ConEd dashboard: a feature labeled "buildings near the LL97 penalty threshold" is probably *not* labeling buildings most likely to disconnect. It's more likely labeling buildings that will (a) negotiate green-steam offtake with ConEd as ConEd electrifies its steam plants, (b) pay LL97 penalties, or (c) buy RECs / use offsets — all behaviors invisible to a disconnect-prediction model.

Recommendation: do not treat threshold proximity as a strong direct churn signal. At minimum, it needs to be conditioned on factors that make disconnection physically and economically feasible — e.g., steam used only for space heat (not process / sterilization / humidification), building size/age that supports a heat-pump retrofit, ownership structure that can finance capex, and absence of a ConEd green-steam offer.

### For the main model (ll97_penalty_2030 weighting)

The Boston evidence supports **downweighting `ll97_penalty_2030` as a direct churn predictor** and reframing it as one input in a multi-step "feasibility-conditioned" pressure signal:

- It should not be a standalone large coefficient. The Boston pattern shows that "high penalty exposure" maps more often to "buys RECs / pays ACPs / waits for green steam" than to "disconnects."
- It should be interacted with feasibility variables: steam end-use mix (process loads are sticky), building type (hospitals, labs, hotels are stickier than offices), capital availability, and ConEd's own green-steam offer timeline. A high-penalty hospital is almost certainly not disconnecting; a high-penalty mid-size office with steam-only space heating *might*.
- It should also be interacted with the question of whether ConEd offers (or plans to offer) a BERDO/LL97-recognized low-carbon steam product analogous to eSteam. If so, penalty pressure largely routes back to the utility, not to disconnection.

Without those conditioning factors, the feature risks high false-positive rates: flagging the buildings most exposed to LL97 dollars as "likely to disconnect" when they're really likely to pay the penalty, buy RECs, or stay on for a future green-steam product.

## Gaps

What could not be determined from public sources:

- Quantitative breakdown of how covered Boston buildings split across compliance pathways. The first BERDO compliance filings are only just arriving (Aug 2026 deadline) and haven't been analyzed publicly.
- Whether any Vicinity Boston customer has actually disconnected — Vicinity does not publish customer churn, and trade press has not reported a named case.
- How small-commercial Boston steam customers behave under BERDO (most coverage is institutional).
- The cost stack of eSteam vs. retail electricity for a heat-pump retrofit at a representative Boston customer. POWER Magazine claims Vicinity gets wholesale rates that can be half of retail, suggesting eSteam is competitive, but I did not see a Boston-specific apples-to-apples cost analysis.
- The internal ConEd customer-base equivalent: even rough year-over-year disconnect counts since 2019 would be the most useful single dataset for validating or rejecting the threshold-proximity feature, and is held by ConEd. This is the dataset to ask for.
- Direct empirical work on "BPS → district-steam disconnection" rates — I did not find an academic or RMI/ACEEE study that has actually attempted to measure this. RMI's REALIZE-MA work focuses on residential / multifamily envelope retrofits, not commercial district-steam dynamics.

What would settle it:

1. ConEd's own customer-level disconnect data joined to LL97 exposure (the most direct test).
2. Boston's first full BERDO compliance dataset (available late 2026/2027) showing pathway choice by building type.
3. Vicinity customer count year-over-year since 2021, if it can be obtained via IDEA filings, state PUC filings, or direct request.
